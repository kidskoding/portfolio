It took 73 hours.

It took 128 attempts.

And the final blow went to the player who died three times in his first two minutes.

That's how Jynxzi, StableRonaldo (Ron) and LosPollos (Los) beat hardcore Minecraft earlier this month, live, without ending the stream. It sounds like the furthest thing from a lesson about work, but **it's one of the clearest examples of a great team I've seen this year.**

I'm spending this fall as a software development engineering intern at Brunswick, after a summer at T-Mobile, and I think a lot about what separates the teams people love working on from the ones they put up with. **This stream is one of the clearest answers I've seen**, and it comes down to four lessons.

---

## Meet the team

If you don't follow streaming, here's who these three are.

### Jynxzi

Jynxzi became one of the biggest streamers on Twitch playing Rainbow Six Siege, a tactical shooter, on an Xbox controller, a setup most top Siege players skip in favor of a PC. **His loud, unfiltered reactions to the game turned him into a phenomenon**, and in 2023 he passed streaming giants like xQc and Kai Cenat to hold the most active subscribers on all of Twitch. At the 2024 Streamer Awards he won both Gamer of the Year and Best Breakthrough Streamer.

### StableRonaldo (Ron)

Ron has streamed on Twitch since 2018 and became one of the most popular Fortnite streamers there, with millions of followers. He also played Fortnite competitively for NRG and FaZe Clan, and he was Jynxzi's partner in the hardcore run they beat together in May.

### LosPollos (Los)

Los is a variety streamer who first built his audience on Twitch with NBA 2K, Call of Duty and Fortnite, and now streams mainly on Kick. **He's known for chaotic, funny streams more than for grinding one game**, which is what makes his role in this story so good.

## Lesson 1: Great leaders design the win around someone else

### On the stream

Before the marathon, Jynxzi announced the rules: no enchantments, no totems (the item that saves you from one death), and one rule that changed everything. **Los had to deal the main damage to the Ender Dragon.**

<TweetEmbed url="https://x.com/scubaryan_/status/2107232155319545965" />

That's a strange rule if all you care about is winning, especially once the run started with Los dying three times in under two minutes. A team optimizing for the fastest clear would have let its strongest player take the dragon. Instead, **Jynxzi set the goal up so the win would belong to the person most likely to struggle with it**, and the job of the other two became getting Los to the point where he could beat it.

### At work

In a lot of workplaces, the strongest person takes the hard ticket, gives the big demo and fixes the outage at 2 AM. That gets things done, but the rest of the team never gets the reps, and **the team ends up only as strong as its single best person.**

The leaders worth following do what Jynxzi did. They give the launch demo to the person who built the feature, let a newer engineer drive the incident call while they sit next to them, and hand off the interesting problem with enough context to actually finish it. As an intern, I've noticed the engineers I learn the most from work exactly like this: they could take the hard task in half the time, but they'd rather stay close and let me take it.

> The goal isn't to be the person who kills the dragon. It's to build a team where anyone can.

## Lesson 2: Shared ownership beats individual scorekeeping

### On the stream

The stream never tracked who died the most. It tracked **128 attempts for the group**, and with 127 deaths across those attempts, every death, no matter whose, sent all three of them back to a fresh world with nothing. Nobody could win while someone else lost.

### At work

Real projects work the same way. When a deploy breaks production, it doesn't matter much whose commit it was, because everyone is rolling back together. **Teams that handle this well ask what made the mistake easy to make, not who made it.** That's the idea behind a blameless postmortem, and it's why good teams talk about "our outage" instead of "your bug."

When ownership is shared, people stop protecting their own score and start protecting the run. **That shift is what turns a group of individuals into a team.**

---

## Lesson 3: Judge contributions by data, not reputation

### On the stream

The funniest moment of the marathon came after it ended. A viewer went back through the deaths and found that **Jynxzi, not Los, was the weakest link**, and Los couldn't believe it. Everyone, including Los himself, had assumed the newcomer to the run was the problem.

### At work

That bias shows up on real teams constantly. The new hire, the intern or the quiet engineer gets quietly labeled as the risk, while the senior person's mistakes get explained away. **The person a team assumes is holding it back often isn't**, and you only find that out if you look at the run instead of the reputation.

For me, this is also a reminder from the other side of the table. As the newest person on a team, **my reputation is something I build with results**, so I want the work to speak for itself.

---

## Lesson 4: Resilience is a skill, and it's fine to let people see it

### On the stream

Jynxzi had already lived through the worst of hardcore. In May, during a marathon with just Ron, he swung at an End Crystal at point-blank range more than 11 hours into an attempt, with Ron yelling at him not to. **The explosion ended the run, and he teared up on stream.** He kept playing, finished that marathon with Ron on attempt 78, and then signed up to do it all again in October with rules that made it harder.

### At work

Everybody has a version of the End Crystal: a release that rolled back, a project that got cancelled, or an interview where you froze on a problem you knew. **Setbacks aren't the opposite of progress, they're part of it.**

What stood out to me is that Jynxzi didn't hide the moment. He's one of the biggest streamers in the world, a Gamer of the Year at the 2024 Streamer Awards, and he let a huge audience watch him get upset and then get back up. **When people with influence show their setbacks, everyone around them feels safer taking risks**, and that kind of psychological safety is what lets a team try hard things.

> Everybody has a version of the End Crystal. What matters is what you do with the next attempt.

---

## What I'm taking into my career

This stream gave me a short list I want to hold myself to as I grow as an engineer:

- **Set other people up to win.** Share context, share credit, and give away the demo when someone else did the work.
- **Own the outcome together.** Treat every incident as the team's, and focus on what made the mistake possible.
- **Look at the data before the reputation.** Including my own assumptions about who's struggling and who isn't.
- **Get back up in public.** Talk openly about what went wrong and what I'm doing differently on the next attempt.

---

## The kind of teammate I want to be

Most of that stream was chaos, deaths and three friends roasting each other. **But underneath it was a real lesson in leadership: decide what winning means for the team, then build it around the person who needs the win the most.**

That's the kind of teammate I want to be, and the kind of team I want to build.

It took 73 hours and 128 attempts.

Ron and Jynxzi could have taken the dragon themselves.

They let Los hit it instead.
